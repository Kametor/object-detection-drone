02 / 22Motivation

Problem Definition & Real-World Motivation

This isn't an object-detection problem — it's object detection under label scarcity. That constraint is the actual, recurring situation in the field, not an artificial one.

AThe assignment

  • Detect and localize one class in drone video
  • 13 short videos. Little data, no ground truth at all.
  • Full manual labelling out of scope
  • Real question: build and validate without labels?

BWhy this matters in practice

  • Surveillance networks: hundreds of cameras, thousands of detections
  • A new product on the line → a class no model was trained on
  • Industry 5.0: product counting, safety, quality inspection
  • Data never arrives labelled; hardware budget is limited

Thermal camera flags a fence intrusion, tracks its path.

Bottling-line QC — one defective unit flagged.

The task itself is straightforward: detect an object in drone video. What makes it hard is the constraint — thirteen short videos, no ground truth, and labelling everything is explicitly out of scope.

This is not an artificial constraint. Think of a surveillance network with hundreds of cameras. Or a production line where a new part is introduced — a class no model was ever trained on. Data never arrives labelled, and we cannot annotate everything.

So the real question becomes: how far can we get without labels, and how do we know that what we built actually works?

Target Selection

We didn't pick the target blindly — we tested every candidate against explicit criteria, recorded what got eliminated and why, and person won on all four.

  • Recorded per video: duration, objects present, object size, background, occlusion

BSelection criteria, checked against every candidate

Before choosing anything, I went through all thirteen videos systematically and built an inventory: what objects appear, what the background looks like, the viewpoint, the duration.

Then I checked every candidate class against four criteria: does it appear in several videos, does it show real scale variation, are there enough instances, is there genuine occlusion material. A boat, a vehicle, a sled dog, a statue — each failed at least one. Person passed all four: eleven of thirteen videos, strong scale variation from altitude, easily five hundred-plus instances, rich occlusion material.

It also happens to already be a COCO class, which matters later — it means the very first baseline measures a genuine domain gap, not just an unfamiliar class.

04 / 22Data Preparation

Data Preparation: Splitting Strategy

Split at the video level, never the frame level — and every split deliberately spans different domains. That's what makes the later numbers mean something.

13videos, 7m52s total
11/13contain the target
4/6/1test/train/val videos
2background (person-free)

ASplit at video level — not frame level

  • Frames within one video are near-duplicates, one domain
  • Frame-level split → leaks test domain into training → inflated scores
  • Train / Val / Test: 6 / 1 / 4 videos (of 11 person-containing), plus 2 background videos held out separately

BWhy each split was sized that way

  • Test: enough videos to evaluate in a genuinely general context
  • Train: enough videos to avoid one narrow domain
  • Val: threshold tuning and model selection only, never in final numbers

CDeliberate domain diversity

  • Test spans 4 different domains — grass trail, hilltop monument, ship deck, volcanic terrain
  • Train spans 6 more, non-overlapping — snow ridge, vineyard/lake, snow field, glacial lagoon, rocky summit, grass trail
  • No split leans on near-duplicate footage of one scene

One decision here is more important than it looks. I did not split frames — I split videos. Frames inside a single video are almost identical and share one domain; a frame-level split would leak the test domain straight into training and inflate every number I show you today.

Test had to cover enough videos to evaluate the methods in a genuinely general context. Train had to cover enough not to overfit to one domain. And this wasn't just about counts — test spans four genuinely different domains, train spans six more, and none of them overlap. That's what makes every mAP number later in this talk actually mean something.

Automatic Labelling with SAM 3

A–BWhy automatic, and the pipeline

  • Manual labelling doesn't scale to the training set
  • Prompt-driven segmentation → masks → boxes, imgsz=1024 (1920 OOM'd on T4's 14.56GB)
  • confidence_threshold=0.5 — see the shadow fix below

CValidated against ground truth first

  • Run on the manually labelled test videos too, compared to our own CVAT boxes — only then trusted for training
  • Agreement: mAP@.5 = 0.846 vs. gold test set (Slide 13)
  • Inference: 2.72s/frame mean on T4 (≈0.37 FPS) — from the zero-shot benchmark under matching settings, not logged separately during the labelling run itself
  • 471 frames, 1,411 boxes produced (train+val, post quality filtering)

Failure caught and fixed: a runner's shadow scored 0.47 and got boxed as a person. Threshold raised 0.3→0.4→0.5 to exclude it — deliberately, accepting some risk to genuine hard/small instances elsewhere.

A different video, same discipline: DJI_0790's sled team — 6-8 dogs correctly excluded, only the musher and passenger boxed as person. The dogs were SAM3's other failure mode (Slide 9).

The test set is hand-labelled, but that does not scale to training. So I built an automatic labelling pipeline using SAM 3. Before trusting it on the training set, I ran it on the manually labelled videos too and compared its output against my own annotations.

The interesting part is what went wrong. The pipeline started labelling shadows as people. Raising the confidence threshold removed most of it, at the cost of some recall — a trade I was willing to make, because noisy labels propagate into everything trained on them.

Before training anything, I wanted a number for the raw domain gap — so I ran COCO-pretrained YOLO26n, zero fine-tuning on our data. The weights already know "person" from COCO — that's deliberate, it means I'm measuring a domain gap, not testing an unfamiliar class. Input resolution is 1920, not the default 640, because aerial people are tiny and 640 would erase them before the network even sees them. And I stayed on the nano variant, not s or m — pushing resolution to 1920 already multiplies the compute per image, so the smallest checkpoint is what keeps FPS reasonable. Confidence threshold is Ultralytics' own default, deliberately untouched.

Overall it looks reasonable: point-seven mAP, point-eight F1, about seven frames a second on a T4. But look at the size breakdown. mAP on large objects is point-six-eight. mAP on small objects is point-zero-two — essentially zero. On the two hardest test videos, this detector finds zero of forty-four small, distant people, at its default confidence. But I checked lower thresholds too — twenty-six of those forty-four actually do get a candidate box, just too low-confidence to survive the default cutoff. That's not a dead end, it's exactly the opening the next method uses. Everything I show you next responds to this one number.

Cascade: Architecture & Confidence Selection

Method 1's blind spot motivates a two-stage system — but lowering YOLO's confidence floor alone floods precision with noise, which is exactly why a second, independent model sits downstream of it.

CChoosing the floor — measured, not guessed

  • Swept YOLO's own confidence threshold on val, checked candidates against SAM3 pseudo-labels (IoU≥0.5)
  • 0.25→0.01: recall triples (27.1%→80.4%) but candidates/frame grow >10× (0.56→6.09) — most of that growth is noise, not new true positives

Method 1 exposed one very specific gap: zero small or distant people caught, at any threshold. My response was a two-stage system. Stage one is the same YOLO with its confidence floor dropped way down. I didn't pick 0.01 arbitrarily — I swept the threshold on validation data and measured recall against the pseudo-labels at each point. Recall triples going from the default 0.25 down to 0.01. But candidates per frame go up more than ten times over the same range, and the number of people in a frame obviously isn't growing ten times — so almost all of that extra volume is noise. That's exactly why I needed a second model whose only job is separating the real detections out of that flood.

09 / 22Alternative Method 1 — Cascade · Part 2

Cascade: Training the Verifier

How the second stage's training data and recipe were built — including the most interesting failure this project surfaced.

AHow the training data was built

  • Positive crops: YOLO candidates overlapping a SAM3 person box at IoU≥0.5. Negative: YOLO's own false positives (IoU=0) + background-video crops. Ambiguous zone (0<IoU<0.5): discarded
  • Audit found SAM3 boxing sled dogs as "person" on the snow video — 271 boxes manually corrected to not_person, feeding the verifier the exact confusion that broke the teacher
  • Final: train 4,905 (1,398 person / 3,507 not_person) — val 221 (181 / 40)

BTraining recipe

  • ResNet18, ImageNet-pretrained — plain PyTorch + torchvision
  • Input 64×64 crops (40% context padding) · Adam optimizer
  • 15 epochs, batch 64
  • Augmentation — flip + colour jitter
98.9%Val person F1 (best epoch)
98.2%Val accuracy
15Epochs
64Batch size

Building the training data for the second stage is where I found the most interesting problem in this whole project. Auditing SAM3's own pseudo-labels on the snow and sled-dog video, I found it had been boxing the sled dogs as people — from directly overhead, on white snow, a dog and a person in a coat genuinely look alike. I corrected two hundred seventy-one of those boxes by hand and fed them back into the verifier's training set as "not a person".

On the recipe: ResNet18 pretrained on ImageNet, plain PyTorch, AdamW at a constant learning rate, fifteen epochs over about five thousand crops. The classes are imbalanced, so I weight the loss by inverse class frequency instead of resampling. Augmentation is deliberately light — these crops are already small and blurry. It trained on CPU — small model, small dataset. The best checkpoint hit ninety-eight point nine percent precision and recall on validation.

10 / 22Alternative Method 1 — Cascade · Part 3

Cascade: The Iteration

The first version made things worse, not better. Three measured refinements — each kept or discarded on evidence — got it past the baseline.

  • Verify all boxes → F1 collapses 0.786→0.514: the verifier wrongly rejects boxes Method 1 already had right
  • Band-gate + video-balance → only the ambiguous [0.01, 0.25) band goes through the verifier; one video was ~75% of training crops, fixed by equal per-video weighting. mAP@.5 recovers to 0.760
  • Score fusion → sqrt(yolo_conf × verifier_prob), mAP@.5 → 0.786, biggest single jump this cycle
  • Small-input stem — stock ResNet18 downsamples 64×64 crops to 16×16 before reasoning even starts; swapped for the original ResNet paper's own small-input design (He et al. 2016, §4.2), a 3×3 stem with no maxpool that keeps full resolution

The first version of this made things worse, not better. Verifying every box collapsed F1 from point-seven-eight-six to point-five-one-four. The fix was to trust YOLO above its normal threshold, and only send the ambiguous band to the verifier.

This is the biggest mAP jump of the whole cycle — point-seven-eight-six against Method 1's point-six-nine-three. I'm deliberately not quoting a single-threshold F1 win here: I checked, and if you let both methods search for their own best threshold on the test set, Method 1 actually edges back ahead slightly. That's what happens when you pick a threshold because it flatters the test set — which is exactly why mAP, not a cherry-picked operating point, is the number I'm standing behind.

Resolution fragility: forced to YOLO's 1920×1088, RT-DETRv2-R18 collapses 0.740→0.253, FP jumps to 1733 — it has no NMS at inference, its one-query-per-object behaviour is learned for one resolution. YOLO tolerates this fine.
Both RT-DETR checkpoints resist the DJI_0501 statue trap that fools YOLO and SAM3 (Slide 16) — a measured, architecture-specific difference: self-attention reasons over the whole frame, not just local shape.

Zero-shot, RT-DETR actually ranks detections better than YOLO — but at a real cost: two to four times more false positives at the same threshold. The more interesting finding: forcing it to YOLO's resolution broke it completely, because RT-DETR has no non-max-suppression step at inference. YOLO doesn't have this problem, because NMS is separate and resolution-independent.

Zero training, zero fine-tuning on this project's data — the same model that labelled the training set (Slide 6), now benchmarked under the identical protocol as every other method.

Every method so far is a supervised detector, trained or fine-tunable. This one isn't. It's also the best-performing method I tried, on every ranking and recall metric, with no training at all. The cost is precision and, far more severely, speed. Not a deployment candidate as it stands — but that was never its job.

19 / 22Production System

Proposed Production System

Don't deploy one method — combine the fast one and the accurate one, each doing the job it's actually good at.

  • Real-time path: cascade, every frame — fast, best mAP among trained methods
  • Periodic audit: SAM3, ~1 call/10s — far too slow per-frame, but highest accuracy available
  • If SAM3's reading disagrees sharply with the cascade's recent output, flag the segment for human review
  • Mirrors exactly how both models are already used: SAM3 as teacher (Slide 6), cascade as deployed student (Slides 8-11)

If I had to put one thing into production, it wouldn't be a single model — it would be this combination. SAM 3 runs in the background as a periodic sanity check, not the primary detector. This mirrors exactly how I already used these two models earlier in this project.

20 / 22Limitations

Honest Limitations & Future Work

What didn't get done this cycle, and why, stated plainly.

Already surfaced in this talk

  • Small-object detection: dominant failure mode for every method, none solve it fully
  • End-to-end FPS for YOLO/cascade/RT-DETR on T4: not yet benchmarked
  • Test set: 4 videos — enough for per-condition breakdown, a small statistical sample

Time-boxed out, not forgotten

  • Fine-tuning YOLO26n on our own pseudo-labels — highest-priority item still open
  • RT-DETR fine-tuning — transformers are more data-hungry, judged too risky this cycle
  • ConvNeXt+CenterNet+FPN; TensorRT/FP16 export; YOLO P2 head + SAHI tiling

Explicitly future work

  • Synthetic data (Blender domain randomization, optionally Gaussian splatting)
  • INT8 quantization for edge deployment

I want to be direct about what's finished and what isn't. Every YOLO result today uses the zero-shot checkpoint — fine-tuning it on our own pseudo-labels is the single highest-priority item still open, and it's next.
